Walk into almost any pub in Australia on a Thursday night and somewhere out the back, under a low light, a competition is being decided by a man who is quietly furious.

He lost the first frame on a kick shot that should have been a safety, the second by rushing a thin cut. It's 2–0 against him, he has stopped chalking between shots, and he is doing arithmetic that has nothing to do with the layout: what this defeat means for his week, his entry money, his reputation.

He will probably lose this match. The evidence suggests he was always likely to lose it — for reasons that have almost nothing to do with how well he can pot a ball.

## The arithmetic of a bad year

There is a particular kind of player who works the competition circuit for a living, or close to it: twelve competitions a week, a dozen or more matches across them, entry fees out, prize money in. It is a grind that selects for people who can absorb defeat, because the arithmetic guarantees it: a player who wins exactly half of every match loses six matches a week, around three hundred in a season. Nobody who needs to win to feel alright lasts long on that schedule.

Elite sport has run this experiment, and it fails for everyone. Roger Federer put his own numbers on the record at Dartmouth in 2024: 1,526 singles matches played, almost 80% of them won — and only 54% of the *points*. Ronnie O'Sullivan, the most successful player in the history of snooker, has lost 411 of his 1,594 professional matches. The greatest winners in these sports lose a quarter to a third of their matches, and roughly half of the individual exchanges inside them. A loss is not evidence that something has gone wrong. It is the cost of playing.

Which is what makes the second number so strange: the same player, same cue action, same practice routine, can go through a stretch of losing nine out of ten. Two things are being described in that sentence — **skill**, which did not move, and **the management of results**, which did. The gap between them is the subject of this article.

## What the science does and doesn't say

The honest state of the evidence, up front: **no published study tests how pool or snooker players perform in the match after a bad loss.** The literature offers quiet-eye research using billiards players and one small think-aloud study of professional snooker players — and that is roughly it. Anyone who tells you "studies prove" that a loss wrecks the next match is over-claiming.

The components, though, are well studied elsewhere: how long an emotional state persists, how it bends the decisions you make while it lasts, why errors cost you your rhythm, what pressure does to a skill you have already learned, and what the mathematics of repeated competition says about any single result. Together they describe something trainable. Call it losing properly.

## You are not as bad as your worst week

Start with the noise. Assume, for argument's sake, a player whose true standard is a coin flip: 50% against the field, week in, week out. Play a dozen matches a week — the chart below assumes twelve — and the outcomes cluster around six and six, but "cluster" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. In a twelve-match week, a genuine 50% player has roughly a **2% chance of winning two matches or fewer**, and about a **7% chance of winning three or fewer**.

Those small numbers compound. Across a 52-week season, the same player has around a **64% chance of at least one week at two wins or fewer**, and close to a **98% chance of at least one week at three or fewer**. That is not a collapse in form. That is the shape of a coin.

![How often a genuine 50% player has a catastrophic week](images/learn-to-lose-first/streaks.png)

This is the null model: it assumes nothing about you changes from week to week, which isn't quite true, but it sets the baseline. And the baseline is brutal, because extreme results regress toward the mean — a fact statisticians have understood since Francis Galton.

> A bad week is not evidence of decline. It is what a fifty-fifty player's season looks like.

The danger isn't the bad week. It's the story you tell about it, because acting on that story is how a bad week becomes a bad season.

## Why one loss weighs more than one win

Your brain is not a neutral accountant. In prospect theory — the framework Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky introduced in 1979 — losses are felt more keenly than equivalent gains. The most-quoted estimate, from their 1992 paper, is a loss-aversion coefficient of about **2.25**: a loss stings roughly twice as much as the same-sized win feels good.

That number is famous, and on the best current evidence it is too high. A 2024 meta-analysis by Lukasz Walasek and colleagues pooled the literature at **1.31, 95% confidence interval 1.10 to 1.53** — a real asymmetry, but a modest one, whose interval only just excludes "no asymmetry at all".

The correction changes the instruction. Expect the sting; it isn't a character flaw. But it is not so overwhelming that it excuses the decisions that follow it. **The feeling is not the problem. What the feeling does to your next decision is.**

![The loss-aversion curve, and why the famous number is contested](images/learn-to-lose-first/loss-aversion.png)

## The scoreboard is not the state

The standard model for sequential decisions — the Markov decision process — carries a property that is almost insulting in its simplicity: **the optimal action depends on the current state, not on the sequence of outcomes that produced it.** Given the table as it stands, the best shot is the best shot at 5–0 up or 5–0 down.

Professional basketball offers a large test. In a 2011 study in *Management Science*, Jonah Berger and Devin Pope analysed more than 18,000 professional and 45,000 college games: a team *slightly* behind at halftime went on to win more often than a team slightly ahead — a swing of about six percentage points, roughly half a home-court advantage. Over a small margin, being behind predicted the opposite of losing.

The failure mode has a name in poker, where the research is better: **tilt**. Tilt escalates with the size of the loss — chasing tracks the magnitude of the losses already incurred — and in a survey of 417 poker players, higher sensitivity to losses predicted more severe tilting, with **more experienced players reporting more tilt over the long run, not less.**

Experience is not a vaccine, because tilt is not a knowledge problem. Emotional states change what you effectively want while they are active, and projection bias means you cannot reliably plan your own in-tilt behaviour while you are calm. The fix has to be structural: rules set in advance, not willpower exerted in the moment.

## The maths of chasing, and the honest counter-argument

There is a mathematical reason to distrust the instinct to press when you're behind. In the family of results that includes the Kelly criterion — the 1956 Bell Labs result on how much of a bankroll to stake — every stake above the growth-optimal fraction lowers your compound growth rate, and stake beyond roughly twice it and expected log-growth turns negative. With a genuine edge, you can still be certain to go broke. Over a season, over-betting is not aggression. It's attrition.

Now the counter-argument, because a well-read player will raise it. Dubins and Savage showed that in an unfavourable game with a **hard deadline and a fixed target**, maximising variance maximises your chance of *reaching the target*. Both results are correct — about different objectives. If the goal is a number by Friday night, bold play can be rational; if the goal is still being on the circuit in two years, growth-optimising play is. **Tilt is the mistake of switching objectives without noticing** — playing a career game with a Friday-night policy.

It is also why tournament formats look the way they do. Single elimination is a high-variance estimator of who the best player is; double elimination and round robin give a second draw from the same distribution. Pool already does this: a World Nineball Tour event fields 256 players and runs double elimination down to the last 128 before switching to single elimination — granting every entrant a second chance, and then stopping. Your own season needs the same design.

## An error costs you rhythm, not just a frame

After an error, humans reliably slow down. Post-error slowing was documented by Patrick Rabbitt in 1966 and replicated ever since. The intuitive story — the brain detects the mistake and corrects it — is not what the evidence shows. The slowing follows **surprise, not error**: Notebaert and colleagues found post-correct slowing after infrequent correct trials, and slowing after irrelevant *tones*, with no error at all. Van den Brink, Wynn and Nieuwenhuis added the mechanism: brain oscillations entrained to the rhythm of the task, errors disrupted that entrainment, and the size of the disruption predicted how much participants slowed. An error doesn't just cost you the frame. It costs you the rhythm the next frame depends on.

Worse for good players: Steinborn and colleagues found that **highly accurate performers show the largest post-error slowing and the strongest post-error accuracy decline**. Valadez and Simons are blunt about the headline — post-error trials are **no more accurate** than post-correct trials. Slowing down after a miss does not, on average, buy you accuracy.

Cue sports have a measurable version of this. In a 2002 University of Chicago study, Williams, Singer and Frehlich tracked quiet eye — the final fixation on the target before the movement begins — in skilled and less-skilled **billiards players**. Skilled players held it longer, and it was longer on successful shots than unsuccessful ones; when the researchers experimentally *shortened* it, performance dropped, **regardless of skill level**. In elite golf, quiet-eye duration predicted **43% of the variance in putting**, and the group trained to hold it **kept it under pressure** while the control group's collapsed — worth 1.9 fewer putts per round in competition. A 1999 University of Florida eye-tracking study of 24 pool players reported the same link, as part of a five-step pre-shot strategy: readying, imaging, focusing, executing, evaluating.

One honest limit: quiet-eye training is proven in golf putting and demonstrated in billiards gaze research, **not in competitive pool or snooker**. But the inference is short and unglamorous. A stable pre-shot routine is the only part of the shot you fully control when your hands are not behaving.

## Why pressure attacks a shot you already own

There is a reason a bad loss makes the *technique* feel worse even though the technique didn't change. Pressure damages skills in proportion to how automatic they have become.

Roy Baumeister's 1984 experiments showed that pressure increases conscious attention to the process of performing, disrupting over-learned execution. Beilock and Carr narrowed it: experts under pressure explicitly monitor a normally procedural skill; choking appeared in golf putting but **not** in a non-proceduralised arithmetic task; and it was unchanged by dual-task training but **eliminated by training that reduced self-consciousness**. Rich Masters' reinvestment hypothesis says the same thing from the other direction — under pressure you re-run the shot using the beginner's explicit rules, reintroducing the control the skill had outgrown. A pool shot is slow enough that there is time to think. That is exactly the opening self-monitoring needs.

## What the players who last actually do

Put the pieces together and the player who converts the 90% stretch back into 50% is not a player with better nerves. They have a different relationship to a result.

**They shorten the emotion instead of feeding it.** Emotions don't have fixed durations. In experience-sampling work by Philippe Verduyn and colleagues, an episode lasts longer when the triggering event was important, when its onset was intense, and — the key finding — **when the eliciting event is mentally re-presented during the episode.** Replaying the missed shot keeps the state alive. Same-valence thoughts prolong it; opposite-valence thoughts shorten it; talking it over with others prolongs it; reflecting from a **self-distanced, third-person perspective** shortens it. Telling someone the score is fine. Telling them the story is how you stay in it.

**They make one controllable attribution, and stop.** In Weiner's attribution theory, a loss pinned on a stable, uncontrollable, internal cause — "I can't hold a nerve" — lowers your expectation of future success, while the same loss pinned on something unstable and fixable preserves it. And the original learned-helplessness researchers concluded fifty years later that **they had it backwards**: passivity is the unlearned default, and what is learned is control. You are not teaching yourself helplessness by losing; you are failing to build evidence of control. Which is why the between-match job is to manufacture small, controllable wins rather than promise yourself a better night — Bandura ranked mastery first among the sources of self-efficacy and encouragement last, and pep talks are cheap for that reason.

**They know that being watched is the active ingredient.** The best-controlled tilt experiment — 459 poker players, anger primed in half, half believing they were observed — found that **anger alone did not reduce decision accuracy**. Anger plus the sense of being watched did. A lost match in a pub is lost in public.

**They practise shot preparation and they rationalise.** The one study of professional cue-sport cognition — seven professional snooker players thinking aloud — coded their stressors as table conditions, distractions, and **mistakes**; their coping was **shot preparation** and **rationalising**, precisely what the rest of this evidence recommends. Top players say it in fewer words. Shane Van Boening told *Billiards Digest*: "Whenever you lose, you should learn something." Efren Reyes: "A 100th ranked player can beat a top 10 player." Read as instruction, both are the same advice. A loss is data about one match — not a verdict, and not a forecast.

> Learning to lose properly isn't the consolation prize for players who don't win enough. It's the mechanism by which a good player gets to keep being one.

## A twenty-minute protocol

None of this requires a sports psychologist. It requires deciding, in advance, what happens after the handshake:

- **Between matches, don't replay.** If you review the match at all, review it in the third person, on paper, and write down one unstable, controllable cause. One. A list of causes is rumination with a filing system.
- **Tell people the score, not the story.** Social sharing reliably extends emotional episodes.
- **Re-enter through the routine, not the technique.** Errors disrupt rhythm, and post-error slowing does not buy accuracy. Restore your pre-shot sequence; don't add a correction.
- **Set your stakes before the night starts.** You cannot trust your in-tilt self with a policy decision: entries, side bets and side action fixed in advance, and no escalation on the basis of the current hole.
- **Change the objective out loud.** If the season is the game, a weekly result is a data point. If Friday's purse is the game, say so — then don't complain when the strategy that maximises it costs you the season.
- **Give up on catharsis.** No verified evidence that smashing something improves the next performance; plenty that arousal persists and biases what comes next.

And a caution from the self-improvement aisle: **growth-mindset interventions are not the answer here.** The largest meta-analysis of mindset interventions in education found an effect of d = 0.05 that became non-significant after correcting for publication bias, with the largest effects reported by authors holding a financial interest in the result. Believing you can improve is not the mechanism. Building a routine that survives 2–0 is.

## The two-year view

The twelve-comps-a-week circuit converts a fixed skill level into a wide distribution of results, wide enough to include weeks that look like catastrophe. A player who treats those weeks as verdicts will change their policy — more risk, more entries, more side action, less routine, more tinkering — and will drag a true 50% down toward the 90% their results now confirm.

The player who can lose six times a week and walk back to the table with the same routine, the same stakes and the same quiet eye is not tougher. They are running a better policy: conditioning on the table, not the tally, and treating each loss as a noisy measurement rather than a sentence. That is why the skill is worth naming. Learning to lose properly isn't the consolation prize for players who don't win enough. It is the mechanism by which a good player gets to keep being one.

### What this article does not claim

Journalistic honesty requires the caveats to travel with the findings:

- **There is no direct study of cue-sport performance after a loss.** The evidence here is mechanism plus adjacent domains. Treat the transfer as reasoning, not proof.
- **Psychological momentum is unresolved.** The classic "hot hand doesn't exist" result was shown to contain a statistical bias against finding streaks, but the best-controlled field evidence still finds no performance benefit to being hot even after accounting for defensive pressure. What is well documented is the *belief* in momentum, which is a different thing.
- **Loss aversion is real but modest.** The famous 2.25 coefficient is the most-cited, not the best estimate; the 2024 meta-analytic pooled estimate is 1.31.
- **The chasing maths has a genuine counter-argument.** Bold play is correct when the objective is reaching a target by a deadline. The claim here is about objectives, not about never gambling.
- **Quiet-eye training is proven in golf putting and demonstrated in billiards gaze research** — not yet in competitive pool or snooker.
